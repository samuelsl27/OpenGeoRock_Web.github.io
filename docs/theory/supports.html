3.10

Support forces

Describes OGR Slip2D 0.1.235

A reinforcement — an anchor, a nail, a pile, a geosynthetic sheet, a wall — acts on the sliding mass where it crosses the slip surface. This chapter explains how OGR Slip2D computes that force for its nine support types, how the force is resolved on the base of a slice and introduced into each method as an active or a passive force, and two related calculations: the water thrust in a tension crack, and the back analysis of the force a slope needs to reach a target factor of safety.

3.10.1A support on a slip surface#

A support is a straight segment from its head, the end at the slope face where its plate or connection is, to its tail, the anchored end. OGR Slip2D finds the first point where the segment crosses the slip surface, counting from the head, and evaluates there the force the support can deliver, F(d)F(d)F(d), with ddd the distance from the head to the crossing. The force acts at that point, on the base of the slice that contains it (Figure 3.10.1). Everything is per metre of slope: the capacity of a discrete element is divided by its out-of-plane spacing SSS.

The force, in the direction its orientation gives it (Section 3.10.3), is resolved on the base of that slice, whose chord is inclined at α\alphaα:

A support that never reaches the surface, or that develops no capacity where it crosses it, puts no force on that surface. The factor of safety is then the one of the slope without it, which looks like any other result, so the analysis notes say how many of the supports placed contributed nothing and why.

3.10.2Active and passive supports#

Whether a reinforcement force exists before the mass moves, or develops only as it moves, decides how it enters a factor of safety defined on strength. Following Duncan & Wright (2005), OGR Slip2D offers both readings. An active support, such as a tensioned anchor, is a force already acting on the mass, and it reduces the driving force. A passive support, such as an untensioned nail, a pile or a geosynthetic, is mobilised with the soil, so its tangential force is added to the resistance and divided by the factor of safety like the shear strength. 3.10.3Orientation of the force#

The direction of FFF at the crossing is one of six options:

  • Tangent to slip surface — along the base of the slice crossed, in the sense that resists sliding.
  • Parallel to support — along the support axis from head to tail, the direction in which a bolt in tension pulls the mass. This one is pure geometry: a support whose anchor lies downhill of the slip surface really does push the mass, and the program applies it as drawn.
  • Bisector — the bisector of the two directions above.
  • Horizontal — horizontal, against the direction of failure.
  • Perpendicular to pile — perpendicular to the support axis, on the side that resists sliding.
  • User-defined angle — at a given angle from the positive xxx axis.

The resisting senses follow the failure direction set for the model (Section 3.1.5). Four types also declare a shear capacity — the grouted tieback, the grouted tieback with friction, the soil nail and the helical anchor. When it is not zero, their shear capacity divided by the spacing is added as a second vector, perpendicular to the support axis on the side that opposes sliding, and the resultant replaces the axial force in Eq. (3.10.1).

Head and tail are not interchangeable: the stripping length is measured from the head, the pullout length from the tail, and a parallel force points from head to tail. A support drawn with its head in the stable ground and its tail inside the sliding mass is reported in the analysis notes, because drawn that way round all three are reversed and the factor of safety does not show it.

Interface strength#

Where the bond depends on the stress in the ground, OGR Slip2D samples the support once per analysis in 50 equal segments, from head to tail, and evaluates the interface strength at the middle of each; the integrals above are taken over that profile. The normal stress on the interface is the effective vertical stress at the point — the weight of the soil column above it, plus any ponded water and the vertical pressure of the distributed loads over it, minus the pore pressure — which is the stress of the pullout equation of Berg, Christopher & Samtani (2009). Helical anchors#

A helical anchor carries steel plates along its shaft, and its capacity steps as the crossing passes each plate. The bearing capacity of one plate is that of a circular footing without the self-weight term (Terzaghi, 1943), as used for helices by Perko (2009):

3.10.5Tension cracks#

A tension crack is drawn as a boundary, and the zone above it is soil that cannot carry tension, so no shear strength may be counted along a slip surface inside it. When the crest end of a slip surface lies in that zone, OGR Slip2D cuts the surface at its first crossing with the crack boundary, counting from the crest, and closes the sliding mass with a vertical wall from that point up to the ground surface; the soil between the wall and the slip surface keeps its whole weight (Duncan & Wright, 2005). Only the crest end is cut, because the crack forms at the head of the slide, where the interslice forces go into tension, while the toe is in compression. A surface whose crest lies in the zone but never reaches the boundary lies entirely in soil that cannot resist, and it is discarded.

Water in the crack pushes on the wall with the hydrostatic resultant

Pw=12 γw hw2,horizontal, at hw3 above the base of the waterP_w = \tfrac12\,\gamma_w\,h_w^2, \quad\text{horizontal, at } \tfrac{h_w}{3} \text{ above the base of the water}Pw​=21​γw​hw2​,horizontal, at 3hw​​ above the base of the water

where

hwh_whw​
height of water in the crack, from the crack boundary at the wall up to the water level (m)
γw\gamma_wγw​
unit weight of water, the pore-fluid unit weight of the project (9.81 kN/m³ by default)

The thrust (Terzaghi, 1943) is applied to the outermost slice, next to the wall, and pushes the mass away from the intact ground. The water level is set by one of seven options: dry; filled, which is the default; filled to a percentage of the crack height; filled up to a given elevation; filled to a level a given depth below the top of the crack; at the water table; or at a piezometric line. The level is always clipped to the height of the crack, and a dry crack still truncates the surface.

In OGR Slip2D

The crack is drawn with Boundaries → Add Tension Crack and its water is set in Properties → Define Tension Crack; one crack boundary per model is used. A separate mechanism, for circles only: a circle whose ground entry point lies above its own centre would overhang, and with the search option that creates tension cracks on such reverse-curvature circles (on by default) it is ended by a vertical crack where it turns vertical. That crack is a consequence of the search geometry and is always dry (Section 3.4).

3.10.6Back analysis of the required support force#

The back analysis turns the question round: instead of computing the factor of safety of a reinforced slope, it fixes the factor at a target FtF_tFt​ and solves for the horizontal force TTT that would have to be added, at a chosen elevation, to reach it. With the factor fixed, the resisting and driving sums of the method can be evaluated directly, and Eq. (3.10.2) and Eq. (3.10.3) give the force in closed form, with no iteration:

Since Tpas=Ft TactT_{pas} = F_t\,T_{act}Tpas​=Ft​Tact​, the passive force is the larger of the two for any target above one, and the two coincide at Ft=1F_t = 1Ft​=1, an identity the program's tests check. Negative values, for a surface that already stands above the target, are reported as zero. Every valid surface of the search is back-analysed and the one that needs the largest force is reported, with both values. In OGR Slip2D

Support → Back Analysis of Support Force takes the target factor (1.3 by default), the elevation of the force and the method: Bishop's simplified method, on circular surfaces only, or Janbu's simplified or corrected method. The sums are the method's own, with the supports already in the model applied as the method applies them, so the force found is the one to add to the existing reinforcement. Composite surfaces are left out and counted in the notes. The calculation neither uses nor changes the results of the stability analysis.
