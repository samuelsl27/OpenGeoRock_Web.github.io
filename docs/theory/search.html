3.4

Slip-surface search

Describes OGR Slip2D 0.1.235

The factor of safety of a slope is the lowest one over every slip surface the ground could fail along, and no formula gives the surface that has it. OGR Slip2D finds it by searching: it generates trial surfaces, computes the factor of safety of each and reports the lowest. This chapter describes the seven search methods, the controls that narrow or filter what they try, the optimisation that refines a non-circular result, and how to check that a search has found the minimum.

3.4.1Why the critical surface has to be searched for#

A circular slip surface is fixed by three numbers, the centre (xc, yc)(x_c,\ y_c)(xc​, yc​) and the radius RRR; a non-circular one by the coordinates of its vertices. The factor of safety FFF of Section 3.1 is a function of those numbers, and the quantity a slope stability analysis reports is its minimum over the surfaces considered,

Fc=min⁡s ∈ SF(s)F_c = \min_{s\,\in\,\mathcal{S}} F(s)Fc​=s∈Smin​F(s)

where

S\mathcal{S}S
set of kinematically admissible trial surfaces the search generates
FcF_cFc​
factor of safety of the critical surface, the lowest found

F(s)F(s)F(s) has no closed form, it changes abruptly where a surface starts to cross another layer or the water table, and it often has several local minima: a shallow mechanism in the face of the slope and a deep one through a weak foundation can both be minima, each surrounded by surfaces with higher factors. A method that only walks downhill from one starting surface finds the minimum of the basin it starts in (Greco, 1996). Every search therefore combines two things in different proportions: coverage of the space of plausible surfaces, so that no basin is left unvisited, and refinement of the best region it finds (Cheng et al., 2007).

The result of any search is the lowest factor among the surfaces it tried. It is the global minimum only if the search reached the surface that has it, and Section 3.4.8 describes how to check that it did. Table 3.4.1 summarises the seven methods.

In OGR Slip2D

The surface type and the search method are chosen in Surfaces → Surface Options…. Each method of slices selected in the project runs its own search, so different methods can return different critical surfaces. Grid and Auto Refine are deterministic; the other five draw random numbers from the project's seed (Analysis → Project Settings…, Random Numbers, pseudo-random with seed 10116 by default), so a run can be repeated exactly. Every search evaluates its candidates through the same two functions, evaluate_circle and evaluate_surface, which is where the filters of Section 3.4.6 and the admissibility checks of Section 3.3.4 to Section 3.3.6 are applied, so they act identically on every search, on the optimisation and on the probabilistic re-evaluations. When a seismic analysis mode asks for the critical seismic coefficient, every search minimises kyk_yky​ instead of FFF (Section 3.9).

3.4.2Circular surfaces#

The slip surface of a circle is its lower arc between two consecutive points where it crosses the ground. A circle that crosses the ground more than twice defines several separate sliding masses, as on a benched slope; each is analysed and the one with the lowest factor is kept. Two cases need a rule of their own:

  • Reverse curvature. When an end of the arc lies above the centre, the arc would turn back over itself. By default a dry vertical tension crack is placed where the arc becomes vertical, at xc∓Rx_c \mp Rxc​∓R, and the mass ends there; the option Create tension crack for reverse curvature can be cleared to discard such circles instead.
  • Leaving the soil. A mass whose arc passes below the lower edge of the External Boundary is discarded, because there is no soil there, unless Composite Surfaces is on (Section 3.4.4).

A trial circle whose factor of safety comes out below 0.2 or above 100 is counted as invalid by every circular search.

Slope search#

Instead of placing centres, the slope search draws circles that are sure to daylight on the slope. It locates the slope face, the steepest run of the ground profile, with its toe, crest, inclination β\betaβ and horizontal width www, and for each trial surface draws at random

  • an exit point on the ground between 0.8w0.8w0.8w beyond the toe and 0.6w0.6w0.6w into the face;
  • an entry point on the ground between 0.4w0.4w0.4w below the crest and 1.5w1.5w1.5w beyond it;
  • the inclination θ\thetaθ of the arc at the exit point, between the Lower and the Upper Angle.

Both points are kept within the ground profile. The circle through the two points with that tangent at the exit has its centre where the normal to the tangent at the exit meets the perpendicular bisector of the chord. It is rejected if the centre lies below either point, if the two points are less than 0.5 m apart horizontally, or if its radius exceeds 50 times the height of the model. The 8 circles with the lowest factors then go through a local refinement of 120 random steps each, moving the centre and the radius by up to max⁡(0.15R, 1 m)\max(0.15R,\ 1\ \text{m})max(0.15R, 1 m), a range that shrinks linearly to zero over the steps, and keeping every step that lowers the factor.

The Slope Limits do not steer where this search draws its points; they still filter the surfaces it finds, as for every search, and the analysis notes say so when limits are set.

Auto refine search#

The auto refine search narrows the search, iteration by iteration, to the parts of the slope that produce the lowest factors. At each iteration:

  1. The search area, at first the slope surface between the Slope Limits, is divided into ddd divisions of equal length measured along the ground, and the midpoint of each is taken on the ground.
  2. For every pair of divisions, ncn_cnc​ circles are drawn through the two midpoints. With ℓ\ellℓ the chord between them and ω\omegaω its inclination, each circle is fixed by its half central angle θ\thetaθ, the angle between chord and tangent, spaced evenly over [1∘, 90∘−∣ω∣−1∘][1^\circ,\ 90^\circ - |\omega| - 1^\circ][1∘, 90∘−∣ω∣−1∘], from an almost flat circle to one whose centre is level with the upper point:
    R=ℓ2sin⁡θ,h=ℓ2tan⁡θR = \dfrac{\ell}{2\sin\theta}, \qquad h = \dfrac{\ell}{2\tan\theta}R=2sinθℓ​,h=2tanθℓ​
    with the centre on the upper side of the chord at distance hhh from its midpoint.
  3. Each division is ranked by the lowest factor of safety among the circles that end in it, and the best fraction of the divisions is kept. The divisions kept, joined into contiguous runs, become the search area of the next iteration; the discarded ones stay discarded.

Every iteration generates

Nit=(d2) ncN_{it} = \binom{d}{2}\,n_cNit​=(2d​)nc​

circles, 450 with the defaults, and the full number of iterations always runs. Ranking a division by the lowest factor of its circles rather than by their average is a deliberate choice recorded in the source: the average was measured to favour the divisions in the middle of the slope, whose circles reach every other division, and failed to move the search towards minima at the ends of the slope.

The non-circular variant generates the same circles and refines the same way, but converts each circle into a polyline of NvN_vNv​ vertices at equal central angles along its arc and computes the factor of safety of the polyline. Every vertex is a mandatory slice boundary, so the number of slices must be at least Nv−1N_v - 1Nv​−1 or every surface is refused; the analysis warns when that is the case. Optimisation (Section 3.4.7) is on by default for this variant.

Particle swarm search#

Particle swarm optimisation (Kennedy & Eberhart, 1995) moves a population of candidate solutions through the search space, each one drawn towards the best positions found so far. where

best position found by the whole swarm, and by particle iii

The update has no inertia weight and no constriction factor, unlike the canonical algorithm. A particle that leaves the cube is reflected back into it, and one whose circle cannot be analysed keeps its place with an infinite factor of safety, so the swarm keeps its size. Drawing each particle towards its nearest neighbours instead of towards the global best is what lets the swarm settle on several minima at once (Qu, Suganthan & Das, 2013). With the enhanced algorithm, the default, the fifth of the swarm with the highest factors is relocated at random at the end of each iteration, so that a swarm collapsed onto one basin keeps probing elsewhere.

To report several minima the final positions are grouped by the species-seed procedure of Li (2004): sorted by factor of safety, the best ungrouped particle starts a group and takes every particle within the grouping radius of it, and so on. The radius is a percentage of the diagonal of the unit cube. Two mechanisms with similar factors in different parts of the slope are two answers; the factor of safety plays no part in the grouping.

In OGR Slip2D

The particles are always circles, and the search is offered under both surface types. Optimise Surfaces is on by default for it: each reported minimum is converted into a polyline of 13 points on its own arc and optimised (Section 3.4.7), so the surface reported can be non-circular even when the surface type is Circular. With several minima requested and optimisation turned off, the analysis notes warn that the search is then hunting local minima, and the lowest of them need not be the global one. The relocated fraction of 20 % is the program's own choice.

Source: ogr_slip2d/particle_swarm.py

3.4.3Non-circular surfaces#

A non-circular surface is a polyline from the ground to the ground, single-valued in xxx. Each of its vertices, and each material boundary or water table it crosses, is a mandatory slice boundary, so a surface with more segments than the number of slices allows is dropped, and the analysis notes say how many were. A polyline has no centre about which to take moments: the methods that use moment equilibrium take them about the point set with Surfaces → Slope Limits → Define Moment Axis…, or, by default, about a point built for each surface from its entry–exit chord, displaced from the midpoint of the chord by the chord itself rotated through 90° (Eq. (3.2.8)).

Moment axis

The program's source records (D47) that a method satisfying moment equilibrium only depends on where that point is. On one verification surface a polyline inscribed in the critical circle gave, with the automatic axis, factors 4.7 % (Ordinary) and 4.2 % (Bishop) below the circle, however finely it was divided, while Spencer, which also satisfies force equilibrium, stayed within 0.1 % of it. Compare a non-circular result with Spencer or GLE/Morgenstern–Price (Section 3.2.5, Section 3.2.6).

Block search#

The block search builds surfaces of the sliding-block type, an active wedge, a central block and a passive wedge, from objects drawn on the model where the user expects the surface to run. Each object gives every trial surface one or more vertices:

  • a window, any closed polygon, gives one vertex at a random point inside it;
  • a line gives one vertex at a random point along it; the surface does not follow the line;
  • a point gives itself, with no random number drawn;
  • a polyline gives two points along it, each drawn anywhere on it, on its end segment or fixed at its end vertex, as chosen separately for the left and the right point, and every vertex of the polyline between them: the surface follows the polyline between its two points, as along a weak seam.

The vertices are sorted by xxx. From the leftmost a straight segment rises at the Left Projection Angle until it meets the ground, and from the rightmost one at the Right Projection Angle; both are measured counter-clockwise from the positive xxx axis, 135° and 45° by default (Figure 3.4.3). Either angle may be given as a range, from which a value is drawn for each surface. A candidate is discarded if a vertex lies above the ground or outside the External Boundary, if an end does not daylight within the Slope Limits, or, with Convex Surfaces Only, if the polyline is not convex. No other object may overlap the horizontal extent of a polyline object, so that its stretch is never cut by a vertex from elsewhere.

With Multiple Groups on, the objects are partitioned by their Group ID and the search runs once per group, using only that group's objects, with the Number of Surfaces divided equally between the groups (the remainder goes to the first); the critical surface is the lowest of all groups. Its intended use is several weak layers searched at once, one polyline per layer.

In OGR Slip2D

A window is sampled by rejection over its bounding box, 50 tries, after which the mean of its vertices is used. The ground crossing of each projected end is found by stepping along the ray in increments of 0.5 model units. When no Block Search object is drawn, OGR Slip2D falls back on a region of its own choosing: a box over the slope face, from 0.3 face widths before it to 0.5 beyond and from 5 % to 75 % of the model height, tiled into the Implicit Region Bands, with one random vertex in each band at least 10 % of the model height below the ground. This fallback is not a documented form of the method, and the analysis notes say so on every such run.

Source: ogr_slip2d/search.py (BlockSearch) · ogr_core/geometry/block_object.py

Path search#

The path search grows irregular surfaces segment by segment from the toe, in the manner of the random generators of irregular surfaces introduced by Boutrup & Lovell (1980) (Figure 3.4.4):

  1. Initiation. A point is drawn at random on the ground in the half of the Slope Limits nearer the toe, or, with two windows of limits, in the window nearer the toe.

Simulated annealing search#

The simulated annealing search treats the vertices of one polyline as the variables of a global optimisation, following the hybrid method of Su (2009): a global phase of very fast simulated annealing (Ingber, 1989), followed by a local Monte Carlo refinement (Greco, 1996).

3.4.4Composite surfaces and weak layers#

Composite surfaces#

A circle that dips below the lower edge of the External Boundary is discarded, because there is no soil there to shear. With Composite Surfaces on, it is instead clipped to that edge, so that a bedrock horizon modelled as the base of the External Boundary guides a circular search:

ys(x)=max⁡(yarc(x), ybase(x))y_s(x) = \max\left(y_{arc}(x),\ y_{base}(x)\right)ys​(x)=max(yarc​(x), ybase​(x))

where ybasey_{base}ybase​ is the lower envelope of the External Boundary. The maximum follows the base wherever the arc dips below it, however many times. On the stretches along the base the normal to a slice no longer passes through the centre, so moments are taken with the general equilibrium of Fredlund & Krahn (1977), about the centre of the circle. The option appears in the panels of the Grid, Slope and Auto Refine searches and is off by default; while it is on it applies to every circle analysed.

Weak layers#

A weak layer is a line drawn with Boundaries → Add Weak Layer and given a material: a joint, a bedding plane or a geomembrane interface, along which a slip surface can run instead of cutting across it. It never divides the model into regions. Any trial surface of any search that a weak layer reaches is replaced by

ys(x)=max⁡(y0(x), max⁡w ∈ W yw(x))y_s(x) = \max\left(y_0(x),\ \max_{w\,\in\,\mathcal{W}}\ y_w(x)\right)ys​(x)=max(y0​(x), w∈Wmax​ yw​(x))

where

y0y_0y0​
the trial surface as generated
W, yw\mathcal{W},\ y_wW, yw​
the active weak layers and their elevation

so that the surface runs along a layer wherever the layer is above it, with the strength of the layer's material there, and returns to the trial surface, or climbs to a higher layer, where it crosses it again. Which layers are active is decided by Weak Layer Handling in Surface Options:

  • Always snap to highest layer (default): every layer that touches the surface is active, and the surface follows the highest; one evaluation per surface.
  • Automatic case generation: every combination of the layers that touch the surface, active or not, is evaluated, including none, and the lowest factor is kept; 2n2^n2n evaluations for nnn layers. A layer is considered to touch the surface if it would clip it on its own. Above the Most layers to combine (6 by default, so 64 cases) the surface falls back to the highest layer, and the analysis notes say so.

Where a layer ends, the surface drops back to the trial surface down a vertical step. A clipped surface is discarded if any slice base is steeper than the Maximum slice base angle (80° by default, Project Settings → Advanced); a value of 90° or more, or of zero, removes this ceiling. It applies only to surfaces a weak layer has clipped. On every surface, for the methods it screens, the m-alpha check acts as a ceiling of about 78.5° on bases in a material with ϕ=0\phi = 0ϕ=0 (Section 3.3.5).

In OGR Slip2D

Weak layers are applied inside the single evaluation step every search uses, so a model without weak layers evaluates exactly the surfaces it generates. The analysis warns about a weak layer without a material, which would change the geometry of the surfaces but carry no strength of its own, and about one lying entirely above the ground.

Source: ogr_slip2d/weak_layers.py · ogr_slip2d/surface.py

3.4.5Focus objects#

A focus object restricts a search to surfaces that interact with a region the engineer cares about: a weak layer, a structure, a point where a crack was observed. There are four kinds, created from Surfaces → Focus Search, and a surface must satisfy every enabled object. The tests depend on what the search generates (Table 3.4.9): a circle is judged as a whole circle, by its centre and radius, and a polyline by its vertices and segments.

Focus objects filter the trial surfaces before their factor of safety is computed, which is what makes them cheap: rejecting a surface costs a few distance calculations, evaluating it a full slicing and iteration. They act in every search, at generation; the walks of the slope-search refinement, the simulated annealing and the optimisation never take a step that leaves them, and a particle of the swarm that leaves them scores as invalid. Surfaces removed by a focus are counted apart from invalid ones, so the populations of a focused and an unfocused run stay comparable. Surfaces the user defines by hand are not filtered.

In OGR Slip2D

A focus object narrows the surfaces a search keeps; it does not generate them, so a Focus Point or a Focus Tangent does not reduce a grid search to one circle per centre, as a generating rule would. The tangency test for a polyline, touching the line without crossing it, is the program's own reading, since tangency is otherwise defined for circles. For the simulated annealing and particle swarm searches, which walk from one candidate to the next, a narrow focus can cut the walk off before it reaches a qualifying surface, and the analysis notes warn of it; a Grid, Path or Block Search reaches a tightly focused region more reliably.

Source: ogr_slip2d/focus.py

3.4.6Slope limits and surface filters#

Slope limits#

The Slope Limits are two abscissae between which a slip surface may daylight. By default they are automatic, at the two ends of the ground profile; Surfaces → Slope Limits → Define Limits… sets them, and a second pair can be added on top of the first to define two separate windows, for instance one at the toe and one at the crest. They do two jobs:

  • Filter. For every search and every surface type, both ends of each sliding mass must daylight inside one of the windows; both ends may lie in the same window.
  • Generation. The searches that build their candidates from the ground use them as the stretch of ground to work on: the grid search for its radii, the auto refine search for its divisions, the path search for its initiation range and segment length, the simulated annealing for the range of its end vertices. The slope search and the particle swarm draw their circles from the slope face instead, and the block search from its objects.

A second window defined without the first is ignored, with a note.

Surface filters#

Three filters in Surface Options discard surfaces before they are solved:

  • Minimum elevation: the lowest point the surface actually reaches, computed exactly from its geometry, may not lie below this elevation.
  • Minimum depth: the greatest vertical distance from the surface to the ground, over all slices, must exceed this value, which removes shallow skins.
  • Minimum area: the area of the sliding mass,
A=∑ibi max⁡(0, hi) ≥ AminA = \sum_{i} b_i\,\max\left(0,\ h_i\right) \ \ge\ A_{min}A=i∑​bi​max(0, hi​) ≥ Amin​

with bib_ibi​ and hih_ihi​ the width and height of each slice. When the filter is not set, each search uses its own value (Table 3.4.10); a value of zero is treated as not set.

Every search except the simulated annealing, which uses 0.5 to 100, also counts as invalid a surface whose factor of safety comes out below 0.2 or above 100. A surface removed by a filter is counted as invalid, so the total number of surfaces a search generated does not change when a filter is set. Besides the filters, a trial surface is also dropped when the slicer cannot divide it into at least three slices with the number of slices set, and a surface that fails the m-alpha or the Tensile Stress Check after its factor of safety has converged is kept in the results but can never be the critical surface (Section 3.3.5 and Section 3.3.6).

Slip surfaces defined by hand, with Surfaces → Add Surface (centre and radius)… or Add Surface (three points), are evaluated with every search of a circular analysis, pass the same filters (but not the focus objects) and compete for the minimum; when one of them is the critical surface, the analysis notes say so.

3.4.7Optimisation of non-circular surfaces#

A non-circular search returns the best of the surfaces it generated, which can usually be improved by moving its vertices. Optimize Surfaces does this with the random walk of Greco (1996): perturb one vertex at random, recompute the factor of safety, keep the change if it lowers it.

  1. The surface is first given 12 vertices, by repeatedly halving its longest segment. Its two end vertices stay fixed.
  2. Each pass visits the movable vertices in random order. A trial position is drawn in a random direction at a distance between 0.2 and 1 times the current step, and accepted if the surface remains single-valued in xxx, no concave angle between adjacent segments exceeds the limit, the surface is valid and admissible, and its factor of safety is lower.
  3. The step starts at 5 % of the length of the surface. After a pass that improves nothing it is multiplied by the Step Reduction Factor, down to 0.2 % of the length.
  4. The walk stops when the factor of safety of the last pass differs from the mean of the last five passes, the current one included, by less than the tolerance, or when the maximum number of evaluations is reached:
∣ Fk−15∑j=k−4kFj ∣<ε\left|\,F_k - \dfrac{1}{5}\sum_{j=k-4}^{k} F_j\,\right| < \varepsilon​Fk​−51​j=k−4∑k​Fj​​<ε

Finally, with Snap Shallow Surfaces to Slope, interior vertices that lie less than a small distance below the ground are moved onto it, the zero-thickness ends this leaves are trimmed, and the surface is evaluated again; the factor of safety can rise as a result.

In OGR Slip2D

The optimisation runs after the search, on the surfaces selected; only the best walk is added to the results, and only if it improves on what the search found, so the report can say that the critical surface came from the optimisation. When the particle swarm reports several minima, each is optimised and kept. The walks use the project's seed, the same for every surface walked. Surfaces → Optimize Surfaces… applies the same optimisation, with the project's settings, to the critical non-circular surface of a method after an analysis.

Source: ogr_slip2d/optimize.py · ogr_core/project/settings.py (optimize_enabled_for)

3.4.8Checking that the search has found the minimum#

No search proves that it has found the global minimum. The following checks, all available in the program, make it likely (Duncan, Wright & Brandon, 2014):

  • Read the analysis notes. They say when the critical centre of a grid lies on its edge, when surfaces were dropped because they could not be sliced or left the model, when a filter or a focus may have removed the mechanism, when the reported surface leaves the ground at 85° or steeper so that its factor depends on the number of slices, and when it rests on an m-alpha below 0.5.
  • Refine the search and compare. A finer grid, more radii, more surfaces or more iterations should not lower the minimum materially. If they do, the search was too coarse.
  • Change the seed. For the random searches, a different seed, or the Random option of the Random Numbers page, should land on a similar minimum.
  • Use more than one search. Compare a circular with a non-circular search, and a grid with a slope or swarm search. The particle swarm with several minima shows whether other mechanisms with similar factors exist elsewhere on the slope.
  • Compare methods. Spencer and GLE/Morgenstern–Price satisfy both equilibria; a moment-only result on a non-circular surface should be checked against them (Section 3.4.3).
  • Test the mechanism you suspect. Define it by hand as a user surface; it is evaluated alongside the search and competes for the minimum.
  • Look at all the surfaces. In the interpretation window, Data → All Surfaces draws every valid surface analysed and Data → Minimum Surfaces the lowest one at each grid centre (Section 2.8); they show whether the minimum is isolated or lies in a broad, well-sampled region.
