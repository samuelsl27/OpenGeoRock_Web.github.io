3.2

Methods of slices

Describes OGR Slip2D 0.1.235

OGR Slip2D implements nine methods of slices. All of them cut the sliding mass into the same slices, apply the same loads and pore pressures and read the strength of each slice base in the same way; they differ in the equilibrium conditions they satisfy and in what they assume about the forces between slices. This chapter gives the equations of each method as the program solves them, with the original source of every formula.

3.2.1Common formulation#

The forces on a slice are those of Figure 3.1.2, and the mobilised shear force on its base is Eq. (3.1.2). In OGR Slip2D the base of every slice is the straight chord between its two base corners, on a circular surface as on any other; α\alphaα is the inclination of that chord, bbb the width of the slice and l=bsec⁡αl = b\sec\alphal=bsecα the length of its base. WWW is the whole vertical load the slice carries: the weight of its soil, the vertical component of any load on its top and the weight of water ponded on it. The pore pressure uuu is taken at the middle of the base.

Each method finds the sense in which the mass slides from the sign of its own driving sum, ∑Wsin⁡α\sum W\sin\alpha∑Wsinα (∑Wtan⁡α\sum W\tan\alpha∑Wtanα for Janbu's methods), and writes its equations in that sense. In this chapter the angles α\alphaα, β\betaβ (the inclination of the ground surface over a slice) and θ\thetaθ (the inclination of an interslice force) are therefore positive where the line dips in the direction of sliding — over the upper, driving part of the surface — and negative where it rises towards the exit. Interslice normal forces EEE are positive in compression, and with θ>0\theta > 0θ>0 the resultant on the up-slope face of a slice pushes it towards the toe and downwards. The equations are written for the static case; seismic, water and support forces enter every method as described in Section 3.9, Section 3.6 and Section 3.10, and the In OGR Slip2D boxes below say where.

3.2.2Ordinary method (Fellenius)#

The Ordinary method, also called the Fellenius or Swedish method, ignores the interslice forces altogether (Fellenius, 1927). The normal force on each base is then the component of the slice's own load perpendicular to it, and moment equilibrium of the whole mass about the centre of a circular surface gives the factor of safety directly, without iteration:

c′, ϕ′c',\ \phi'c′, ϕ′

The pore-water term is u lcos⁡2αu\,l\cos^2\alphaulcos2α and not u lu\,lul. Resolving the effective weight of the slice, W−u bW - u\,bW−ub, perpendicular to the base gives (W−u lcos⁡α)cos⁡α(W - u\,l\cos\alpha)\cos\alpha(W−ulcosα)cosα: this is the correction of Turnbull & Hvorslev (1967). Because its normal force does not depend on c′c'c′ or ϕ′\phi'ϕ′, this is the one method the curved-envelope iteration of Section 3.3.7 leaves alone, and the m-alpha check does not apply to it: it forms no such denominator.

3.2.5Spencer's method#

Spencer (1967) assumes that all the interslice resultants are parallel, at an inclination θ\thetaθ that is not known in advance,

Xi=λ Ei,λ=tan⁡θX_i = \lambda\,E_i, \qquad \lambda = \tan\thetaXi​=λEi​,λ=tanθ

and satisfies both force and moment equilibrium, with FFF and λ\lambdaλ as the two unknowns. OGR Slip2D solves it as the general limit-equilibrium (GLE) formulation of Fredlund & Krahn (1977) with a constant interslice function, so Spencer and GLE/Morgenstern–Price share every equation of this section and differ only in Eq. 3.2.6GLE / Morgenstern–Price#

Morgenstern & Price (1965) let the ratio of the interslice forces vary along the surface as a chosen function f(x)f(x)f(x) scaled by λ\lambdaλ; Fredlund & Krahn (1977) wrote the procedure in the general form of Eq. (3.2.16) and Eq. (3.2.17):

Xi=λ f(xi) EiX_i = \lambda\,f(x_i)\,E_iXi​=λf(xi​)Ei​

With f≡1f \equiv 1f≡1 it is Spencer's method; in OGR Slip2D that identity holds by construction, since the two run the same code. The function is evaluated at the slice boundaries, in terms of the abscissa normalised over the surface,

t=x−x0xn−x0t = \dfrac{x - x_0}{x_n - x_0}t=xn​−x0​x−x0​​

where x0x_0x0​ and xnx_nxn​ are the abscissae of the left and right ends of the surface: t=0t = 0t=0 is the left end whatever the direction of sliding. The program offers the four functions of Table 3.2.3, drawn in Figure 3.2.3. All four are symmetric about t=12t = \tfrac12t=21​, so for them that convention does not depend on which way the slope faces. A function that vanishes at both ends, such as the half sine, allows no interslice shear near them; the clipped sine keeps a ratio of 0.2λ0.2\lambda0.2λ there.

In OGR Slip2D

The function is chosen in Analysis → Project Settings → Methods → Interslice force function, which is active when GLE / Morgenstern–Price is ticked; the default is Half Sine. The range searched for λ\lambdaλ, shared with Spencer, is set on the Advanced page as Minimum lambda and Maximum lambda (−0.1 and 6.0 by default). Only these four functions can be chosen in the program; a Python script that builds the method object itself may pass any function of (x,x0,xn)(x, x_0, x_n)(x,x0​,xn​) (Section 5.3). The result carries λ\lambdaλ and the ratios λf(xi)\lambda f(x_i)λf(xi​) at every boundary.

3.2.7Methods with a prescribed interslice inclination#

Corps of Engineers #1, Corps of Engineers #2 and Lowe–Karafiath satisfy force equilibrium only. Instead of solving for the inclination of the interslice forces they prescribe it from the geometry, which leaves the factor of safety as the only unknown. The three share one engine, the numerical solution of the Modified Swedish method published by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (USACE, 2003), and differ only in where θ\thetaθ comes from.

The inclination of the interslice forces#

  • Corps of Engineers #1 uses one inclination for the whole surface, that of the line joining its two ends. USACE (2003, §C-4a) takes the side forces parallel to the average slope of the embankment, usually the line from crest to toe; OGR Slip2D draws that line between the points where the slip surface meets the ground and, where a tension crack closes the mass, from the top of the crack.
  • Corps of Engineers #2 takes θi=βi\theta_i = \beta_iθi​=βi​, the inclination of the ground surface over slice iii. It is zero wherever the ground is horizontal, so the interslice shear vanishes there.
  • Lowe–Karafiath takes the average of the inclinations of the ground surface and of the slip surface (Lowe & Karafiath, 1960):
θi=12(αi+βi)\theta_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\alpha_i + \beta_i\right)θi​=21​(αi​+βi​)

Each rule defines θ\thetaθ per slice, from the chords of its top (βi\beta_iβi​) and of its base (αi\alpha_iαi​). The force on an interior boundary acts at the average inclination of the two slices that share it,

θi,i+1=12(θi+θi+1)\theta_{i,i+1} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(\theta_i + \theta_{i+1}\right)θi,i+1​=21​(θi​+θi+1​)

Total or effective interslice forces#

Where water pressure acts on the vertical faces between slices, the force transmitted across a face is the sum of an effective part, carried by the soil skeleton, and the integral of the water pressure, which is horizontal. A method that solves for the inclination of the interslice forces, or assumes them horizontal, is insensitive to that split; a method that prescribes the inclination is not. USACE (2003, §C-4a) accepts both readings and notes that the factor of safety differs between them.

In OGR Slip2D

Analysis → Project Settings → Methods → Interslice forces, active when one of the three methods is ticked: Total (water thrust included), the default, or Effective (water thrust separated). With Effective, the water pressure on each vertical face is integrated from the project's pore-pressure model and applied to the two slices that share the face as a horizontal load, and θ\thetaθ is prescribed for the effective force. Total is the convention in which the published values of these methods are given; Effective is the reading to use for a slope with water standing over it, where with total forces the factor of safety depends on the depth of the water.

The march is tried with the geometry as drawn and, if no root is bracketed, mirrored; the first orientation that brackets a root is used. The root refinement takes at most the larger of Maximum iterations and 60 steps. The acceleration of Section 3.3.2 does not apply, and neither does the m-alpha check, since these methods divide by nα(θ)n_\alpha(\theta)nα​(θ) of Eq. (3.2.23) and not by mαm_\alphamα​. The line of thrust is available for all three (Section 3.3.8).

3.2.8Choosing a method#

No method of slices is exact: each closes the statically indeterminate problem of Section 3.1.3 with an assumption. What the methods share and where they differ can be summarised as follows, after Duncan, Wright & Brandon (2014).

  • Complete equilibrium. Spencer and GLE/Morgenstern–Price satisfy every equilibrium condition, and procedures that do so give factors of safety that differ little from one another (Fredlund & Krahn, 1977). Of the methods that use moment equilibrium, they are the only ones whose answer on a non-circular surface does not depend on the moment axis.
  • Bishop's simplified method gives values close to those of the complete-equilibrium procedures on circular surfaces. On other surfaces its result depends on the moment axis (Section 3.2.3).
  • The Ordinary method needs no iteration and always gives an answer, but in effective-stress analyses with high pore pressures its factor of safety can be well below that of the complete-equilibrium procedures.
  • Force-equilibrium methods — Janbu's two and the prescribed-inclination family — depend on the inclination they assume for the interslice forces: horizontal for Janbu's, fixed by the geometry for the others.

Running several methods on the same model, as OGR Slip2D allows, shows how much the answer depends on those assumptions. Table 3.2.4 gives the nine factors of safety on one circle of a simple slope.

Engineering judgement

The spread between methods depends on the problem, and a dry homogeneous slope on a circle is a favourable case. A large spread is a reason to look at the forces on the slices (Section 3.3) before quoting any of the values.
